Ellie Grant was the smartest person in her house, and everyone knew it except her father and brother.

Her father once tried to make toast in the microwave with a metal fork still stuck in the bread. Her brother Max believed fish were “basically wet birds.” They were both loud, lazy, and strangely proud of their worst ideas.

At her cousin’s birthday party, Aunt Nora looked at Ellie across the table and frowned. “Oh no,” Aunt Nora said. “You’re getting that Grant look.”

Ellie stopped eating cake. “What look?” “That blank little stare,” Aunt Nora said. “Your grandfather, your father, and Max all started doing that around your age.”

Ellie felt cold. Her father had a blank stare. So did Max. It usually came right before they said something terrible. Her father once stared at a broken chair and said, “Maybe chairs need vacations.” Max once stared at his homework and wrote, “Because I feel like it,” for every answer.

That night, Ellie checked her face in the mirror. She still had the same double eyelids, the same left eyelash, and the same facial features as yesterday. But did she give off a vibe of stupidity now?

She studied her shoulders. “Is my build changing too?” she whispered, even though that made no sense. That scared her more.

At school, the teacher asked everyone to write about identity and individuality. Ellie wrote, “I used to be smart.” Then she erased it quickly.

By Thursday, Ellie had made a chart called The Grant Stupidity spectrum. On the left side, she wrote normal. In the middle, she wrote confused by doors. On the far right, she wrote Max.

When Max came home selling rocks from the driveway and called it entrepreneurial spirit, Ellie nearly cried.

That evening, she told her mother, “Maybe I should just accept it. Maybe I’m going to become stupid like Dad and Max.”

Her mother put down her cup. “Who told you that?” “Aunt Nora said Grandpa, Dad, and Max all started getting the blank stare around my age.”

Her mother sighed. “Nora left out the important part. That Grant problem only happens to the men.”

Ellie blinked. “Only the men?” “Only the men.”

From the living room, Max shouted, “Mom, is soup a drink or a place?” Ellie smiled for the first time all week. Her brain was still hers.

엘리 그랜트는 자기 집에서 가장 똑똑한 사람이었다. 모두가 그 사실을 알고 있었다. 아버지와 오빠만 빼고.

아버지는 한 번 빵에 금속 포크를 꽂아 둔 채 전자레인지로 토스트를 만들려고 했다. 오빠 맥스는 물고기가 “기본적으로 젖은 새”라고 믿었다.

사촌의 생일 파티에서 노라 이모가 “너도 그랜트 집안 표정이 나오네.”라고 말했다. “그 멍한 표정 말이야. 네 할아버지, 아버지, 그리고 맥스가 모두 네 나이쯤에 그렇게 하기 시작했어.”

엘리는 거울 속 얼굴을 확인했다. 그녀에게는 여전히 어제와 같은 쌍꺼풀, 같은 왼쪽 속눈썹, 같은 이목구비가 있었다. 하지만 이제 자신이 멍청한 분위기를 풍기고 있는 걸까?

그녀는 어깨를 살폈다. “내 체격도 변하고 있는 건가?” 말도 안 되는 생각이었지만, 그래서 더 무서웠다.

학교에서 선생님은 모두에게 정체성과 개성에 대해 글을 쓰라고 했다. 목요일이 되자 엘리는 ‘그랜트 멍청함 스펙트럼’이라는 표를 만들었다.

맥스가 집에 와서 차도에 있는 돌멩이를 팔고 그것을 기업가 정신이라고 불렀을 때, 엘리는 거의 울 뻔했다.

그날 저녁, 그녀는 엄마에게 말했다. “어쩌면 그냥 받아들여야 할지도 몰라요. 저도 아빠랑 맥스처럼 멍청해질지도 몰라요.”

엄마는 한숨을 쉬었다. “그 그랜트 집안 문제는 남자들에게만 나타나.”

거실에서 맥스가 소리쳤다. “엄마, 수프는 음료예요, 아니면 장소예요?” 엘리는 그 주 들어 처음으로 미소 지었다. 그녀의 머리는 여전히 그녀의 것이었다.
