wasian

white + Asian을 합친 비격식 표현이에요. 스스로 정체성을 표현할 때 쓰기도 하지만, 다른 사람에게 사용할 때는 주의가 필요해요.

Suppose we could replace your body one cell at a time.

One cell disappears. A new one takes its place.

Still you?

Obviously.

Ten cells?

Still you.

A million?

I assume so.

So let’s make it harder.

Suppose you get a scar.

Maybe you fell off your bike. Maybe, for some ridiculous reason, someone hit you with a slingshot when you were twelve.

Your body changed.

Still you.

Suppose something much worse happens. A bullet damages part of your body and doctors replace what they can.

Still you?

I think so.

So clearly, being “you” doesn’t require keeping every original piece.

There’s an old analogy about a ship. If you replace every piece of wood one at a time, is it eventually a different ship?

But I don’t love that comparison.

A ship doesn’t wake up running late, forget where it put its keys, and suddenly remember something embarrassing it said in 2014.

You do.

So maybe being you is about memory.

But memories change too.

You forget things. You misremember things. Sometimes somebody tells you a story about your childhood so many times that you’re not even sure whether you remember the event or just remember the story.

Still you?

Probably.

Then maybe it’s personality.

But that changes too.

A shy child can become a confident adult. Someone can move somewhere new, pick up a different dialect, and start speaking differently without deciding to.

Someone might use sign language in one part of their life and spoken language in another.

A wasian person might describe themselves differently at twenty than they did at ten because they understand their own background differently.

Those things can all be part of who someone is.

But none of them seems to be the single thing that makes them them.

And yes, I know where this is heading.

Some terribly corny sentence like, “The real you is on the inside.”

But what does that even mean?

Inside where?

If we keep replacing cells, eventually the inside changes too.

Maybe there isn’t one permanent piece.

Maybe there isn’t a tiny “you” sitting somewhere in your brain, watching everything happen and making sure you remain the same person.

Maybe being the same person simply means that the changes happened continuously.

One moment became the next.

Then the next.

Nothing suddenly announced, “Old you finished here. New you starts now.”

Which is strange.

Because you probably feel like one continuous person.

But the five-year-old version of you had a different body, different thoughts, different fears, different knowledge, and probably a very different idea of what your life would become.

So how different could that person become before you stopped calling them you?

Apparently, extremely different.

And yet there must be something connecting all those versions.

Right?

Or maybe that’s the part we’ve been assuming from the beginning.
